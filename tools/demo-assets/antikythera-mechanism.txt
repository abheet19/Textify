The Antikythera Mechanism: The First Known Analogue Computer

In 1901, sponge divers sheltering from a storm off the Greek island of Antikythera found a Roman-era shipwreck lying about forty-five metres down. Among the bronze statues and amphorae they hauled up was an unremarkable lump of corroded metal, roughly the size of a shoebox. It sat in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens for months before it split apart in the drying air and revealed something nobody expected: precisely cut gear teeth.

The Antikythera mechanism is a geared astronomical calculator built in Greece somewhere between 150 and 100 BC. Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity survives from anywhere in the world for the next fourteen centuries, until the astronomical clocks of medieval Europe. The purpose of the device was not to tell the time. Its job was to predict where the sun, the moon, and very probably the five planets known to antiquity would sit in the sky on any date the user chose, and to say when eclipses would fall.

The mechanism was operated by a hand crank on its side. Turning the crank advanced a date, and a train of at least thirty interlocking bronze gears drove pointers across dials on the front and back faces. The front dial showed the zodiac and the Egyptian calendar. The Metonic dial on the back is a five-turn spiral tracking the nineteen-year cycle over which lunar months realign with solar years, and the Saros dial beside it tracks the eighteen-year cycle that governs the repetition of eclipses.

The cleverest surviving component is a pin-and-slot arrangement of two gears mounted on slightly offset axes. Because the pin of one gear rides in a slot on the other, the driven gear speeds up and slows down over each revolution. The lunar anomaly is the uneven apparent motion of the moon across the sky, and this pair of gears is a mechanical model of exactly that effect. Someone in the second century BC turned a piece of observational astronomy into a piece of engineering.

Understanding of the mechanism advanced in three long steps. Derek de Solla Price, a historian of science at Yale, published the first careful gear analysis in 1974 after taking gamma radiographs of the fragments. In 2005 an international team including Tony Freeth and Mike Edmunds brought an eight-tonne microfocus X-ray computed tomography scanner to Athens and imaged the fragments in three dimensions. The scans read text on interior surfaces that no eye had seen since antiquity, roughly quadrupling the known inscriptions and settling several gear tooth counts that had only been guessed at.

Those inscriptions matter because they work as a user manual. The inscriptions name the dials, describe what each pointer shows, and record the colours and sizes attributed to eclipses. They confirm that the device was meant to be read by someone who was not its maker.

Only eighty-two fragments survive, and together they are perhaps a third of the original. No second example has ever been found. The mechanism is therefore evidence of a lost tradition rather than a lone genius: the workmanship is confident, the design is iterated, and the text is routine in tone. Whoever built it had built things like it before.

What the object changes is the shape of the story we tell about technology. The standard account runs from simple to complex, from antiquity to modernity, in a rising line. A hand-cranked bronze computer from the second century BC, sitting in a shipwreck for two thousand years, does not fit that line. It suggests that sophisticated mechanical knowledge can appear, flourish inside a narrow tradition, and vanish without ever becoming common knowledge.
